- The Cat specification is printed on the cable jacket along with the cable type (UTP or F/UTP, for instance).
- Cat 5 cable supports the older 100 Mbps Fast Ethernet standard. It is no longer commercially available.
- Cat 5e would still be an acceptable choice for providing Gigabit Ethernet links for client computers, but most sites would now opt to install Cat 6 cable.
- Cat 6  is more reliable than Cat 5e for Gigabit Ethernet, and it can also support 10 Gbps, though over reduced range.
- Cat 6A supports 10 Gbps over 100 m, but the cable is bulkier and heavier than Cat 5e and Cat 6.
- Cat 6A instalation is harder to install.
- TIA/EIA standards recommend Cat 6A for healthcare facilities, with Power over Ethernet (PoE) 802.3bt installations, and for running distribution system cable to wireless access points.
- Cat 7 and Cat 8 cables are not yet widely deployed in typical home or office networks.
- They are mainly used in high-bandwidth, short-run installations (for example, in data centers).
- Cat 7's preferred connectors under ISO/IEC standards are GG45 or TERA. While GG45 jacks may accept RJ45 (8P8C) plugs for compatibility, a standard RJ45 jack/plug may not deliver the full Cat 7 specification.
- For Cat 8, there are two sub-standards: Cat 8.1 uses RJ45 connectors (and is backward compatible with typical RJ45 systems), while Cat 8.2 uses GG45 or TERA connectors (and is not backward compatible with standard RJ45 ports).
